Before we start · Purpose of the project

Why are we doing this?

Every engineering project starts with a job to do and a reason it is worth doing. Here are ours.

Reason 1 · The need

Fuel ethanol has to be almost water-free

Ethanol that is blended into gasoline must be anhydrous, and fuel standards set a minimum ethanol content: 98.70% by mass in Europe and 99.30% by mass in Brazil (Miranda et al. 43). Ethanol straight out of fermentation is nowhere near that.

Reason 2 · The obstacle

Plain boiling hits a wall

Ethanol and water form an azeotrope, so simple distillation cannot separate them completely (Miranda et al. 43). A smarter process is needed. Engineers use several: extractive distillation, azeotropic distillation, distillation with salts, pressure-swing distillation and reactive distillation (Miranda et al. 43).

Reason 3 · The choice

Why azeotropic distillation with cyclohexane

In a published Aspen Plus comparison, the azeotropic route reached a higher ethanol purity than the extractive route, though it used more energy (Miranda et al. 43). Cyclohexane has replaced benzene as the helper liquid over the years because it is much less toxic (Miranda et al. 44).

Reason 4 · The method

Test it on a computer before building it

A real plant costs millions of dollars; our early estimate is $4.79 million in capital alone. Aspen Plus lets us try stage counts, reflux ratios and recycle loops on screen, see the purity, energy and cost, and fix problems before any steel is ordered.

1 · The problem

Why would anyone need ethanol with no water in it?

Ethanol is the alcohol that yeast makes when it eats sugar. Factories make it from crops like corn and sugarcane (Miranda et al. 43). What comes out of fermentation is mostly water with some ethanol in it, so the factory has to remove the water.

There are two grades. Hydrated ethanol still contains some water. Anhydrous ethanol (the word means "without water") contains almost none, and that is the grade that gets mixed into gasoline (Miranda et al. 43).

Fuel

Blended into gasoline

Fuel rules set a minimum ethanol content. Europe's EN 15376 asks for 98.70% by mass and Brazil asks for 99.30% by mass (Miranda et al. 43). Our product is 99.92% by mass.

Solvent

Labs and factories

Dry ethanol dissolves things that water would interfere with, so it is a common solvent in laboratories and chemical plants.

Our feed

A 50/50 mixture

Our simulated plant takes in 100 kmol/h of liquid that is half ethanol and half water (counted by molecules) and must return ethanol that is at least 99.95% pure.

How distillation works, in one picture

Distillation separates liquids by boiling. Ethanol boils at about 78 °C and water at 100 °C, so when the mixture boils, the vapor carries more ethanol than the liquid it came from.

A distillation column is a tall tower that repeats this boil-and-cool step on many levels, called stages. Vapor rises, liquid falls, and they meet on every stage. A heater at the bottom (the reboiler) makes vapor; a cooler at the top (the condenser) turns it back into liquid.

Rule of thumb: the stuff that boils more easily ends up at the top. The stuff that boils less easily ends up at the bottom.

2 · The wall

Boiling stops working at 89.6% ethanol

Schematic curve. Its shape is drawn for teaching; the meeting point at 89.6 mol% is the measured azeotrope (Miranda et al. 44).

That meeting point is called an azeotrope. At 1 atm it sits at 78.16 °C, with 89.6% ethanol and 10.4% water by molecules (Miranda et al. 44). There, the vapor has exactly the same recipe as the liquid, so boiling no longer sorts anything. Adding more stages or more heat changes nothing. Ordinary distillation simply cannot reach 99.95%.

The industrial impact

Why this limit matters in a real plant

Distillation columns can concentrate ethanol, but ordinary distillation reaches an azeotropic limit. This is why a real fuel-ethanol facility needs an additional dehydration method.

3 · The trick

Add a third liquid that drags the water away

The helper liquid is cyclohexane, an oily liquid that does not mix with water. Engineers call a helper like this an entrainer. It does not react with anything; it only changes how the mixture boils.

The boiling-point ladder

Mixtures of these three liquids each have their own boiling point. Lowest boils first, so it rises to the top of the column. Tap a rung.

What cyclohexane does, in three moves

  1. It carries the water up. Ethanol, water and cyclohexane together boil at about 62 °C, lower than anything else in the column. That mixture rises to the top and takes the water with it, so the ethanol left behind flows down dry.
  2. It separates itself afterward. When that top vapor is cooled into liquid, it splits into two layers, like oil and vinegar (Gomis et al. 7). A simple tank can pour them apart.
  3. It gets reused. The cyclohexane layer goes straight back to the column, so the plant only tops up the tiny amount that escapes.
The chemistry meets the hardware

What does this look like outside the simulator?

Real plants use tall steel towers rather than the small rectangles in a process diagram. Vapors rise, liquids flow downward, and separation takes place inside each tower. Our simulation models the separation behavior, not the physical appearance of a constructed plant.

6 · Results

Did it work? Everything is accounted for.

Purity vs. recovery

Purity asks how clean the product is. Recovery asks how much of the ethanol we started with made it into the product. Here is the recovery, one step at a time:

  1. Ethanol fed in = 100 kmol/h × 0.50 = 50.000 kmol/h
  2. Ethanol in the product = 50.0206 kmol/h × 0.9995 = 49.9956 kmol/h
  3. Recovery = 49.9956 ÷ 50.000 = 99.991%

The product is 99.95% ethanol by molecules (99.92% by mass). The remaining 0.05% is 0.046% cyclohexane and 0.004% water.

7 · Energy and cost

The price of breaking an azeotrope is heat

Every column needs a heater at the bottom and a cooler at the top. Column 2, where the azeotrope is broken, uses half of all the heat.

For every kilogram of ethanol, the reboilers use about 3.3 kWh of heat. In the water-removal section, our design uses about 111.7 kWh per kmol of ethanol, roughly twice the 52.2 kWh per kmol of a published reference design (Miranda et al. 47).

That gap is the clearest next step: tune the reflux ratios, feed stages and solvent flow to cut the steam bill. Cost estimates are from the Aspen Process Economic Analyzer and are early screening numbers.
